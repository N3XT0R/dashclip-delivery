Almost every current dashcam brings a wireless connection and an app. Recordings can be pulled onto a phone without a card reader, and settings are easier to change than through a small display. Both are practical, and both deserve a second look.

## What the app genuinely makes easier

The biggest gain is quick review. After an incident you can see in the vehicle whether the footage is usable, instead of discovering at home that the camera was pointing the wrong way.

The second gain is the settings. Alignment, sensor sensitivity, segment length and time zone are far more reliably configured in an app than through three buttons and a two line menu.

## How the connection works

Most cameras open their own wireless network for the phone to join. That network often ships with a default password printed in every manual, which makes it effectively public.

Change that password first. While it stays unchanged, anyone within range can connect and, in the worst case, download recordings.

> [!NOTE]
> Switch the wireless connection off when you do not need it. A network left open permanently in the vehicle is an unnecessary point of attack and costs energy as well.

## What apps know about you

Check which permissions a camera app requests. Access to local storage and location is understandable; anything beyond that deserves an explanation.

If an app uploads recordings to online storage, that should be a deliberate decision rather than a default. Dashcam footage contains plates, faces and movement data, and that combination does not belong in other hands without thought.

## Updates arrive the same way

The app is usually also the route through which camera software is updated. That is a good reason to keep it set up, even if you otherwise pull recordings with a card reader. There is more in the article on [camera software updates](/en/blog/camera-software-updates).

## For serious cases, use the card

However convenient the app is, the direct route stays safer for an important recording. A file transferred through an app may be downscaled or re-encoded, and that is exactly what you do not want in a piece of evidence.

In such cases take the card out of the camera and copy the original file unchanged, as described in the article on [rescuing footage](/en/blog/rescuing-footage-before-it-is-overwritten).

## When the connection fails

This is the most common source of trouble with camera apps, and it usually comes down to something small. Check in this order: is the wireless connection switched on at the camera at all? Has the phone joined the camera network rather than another one? Has the phone quietly fallen back to mobile data because the camera network offers no internet access?

That last point explains most cases. Many phones switch back automatically as soon as a network provides no route to the internet. In the network settings that behaviour can be disabled for this one network.

## Not while driving

Operating the app belongs to a stationary vehicle. Reviewing footage at the wheel distracts, and the recording you want to check will still be there five minutes later in a car park.
