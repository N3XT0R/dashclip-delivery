The platform is available in German and English. What that means in practice is less obvious than it sounds, because not everything on a page can be translated.

## What is translated

The interface: menus, buttons, hints, error messages and the text around your submissions. The notifications you receive by email as well. They follow the language set in your account.

The blog appears in both languages too. Articles available in both take you straight to the other version when you switch.

## What stays in its original language

Everything people entered themselves. The description of a channel, the name of a channel, your own description of a clip and any response somebody writes to you.

That is a deliberate decision. A machine translated description often sounds right while meaning something else, and for responses about submissions that would do real harm.

> [!NOTE]
> When you write a description, use the language of the place where the scene happened or the one you express yourself in confidently. A clear description in one language beats an imprecise one in two.

## How to switch languages

Through the language selector in the interface. The setting applies to your session and, where stored, to your account. Page addresses differ per language, so you can share a link in a particular language on purpose.

The blog has one particularity: where an article does not yet exist in the chosen language, you land on the overview rather than on an empty page.

## Submitting bilingually

There is no need to write a description in both languages. Channels work in the language they understand and ask if something is unclear.

It can help to leave place names in the local language and add a short explanation. A street name stays findable that way while the context is still clear.

## For channel operators

If your channel uses material from several countries, a channel description that also reads well in English is worthwhile. It influences whether a submitter judges your channel to be a good fit, as the article on [discovering channels](/en/blog/discovering-channels) describes.

## The blog as a reference

Both language versions of the blog have their own feed. If you collect articles in a feed reader, subscribe to the feed in the language you read, and the entries stay consistent.

## Why two languages at all

Road traffic does not stop at a border. Some channels work internationally, and submitters drive through several countries. A platform that works in only one language shuts part of that out.

At the same time, German language use should not suffer from English terms being left in place. That is why the interface is translated completely rather than partly.

## If a translation stands out

Language errors and unclear wording can never be ruled out entirely. If you come across something ambiguous, a short note with the passage and the language you were using helps. What makes such a report useful is covered in the article on [reporting problems](/en/blog/reporting-problems-and-getting-help).
