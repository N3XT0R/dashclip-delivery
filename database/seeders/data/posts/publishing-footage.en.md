Securing footage and publishing it are two different acts with very different consequences. One stays within your control, the other reaches people you do not know and cannot be undone.

> [!WARNING]
> This article offers general orientation and is not legal advice.

## What is at stake legally

Traffic footage makes people identifiable, and not only through faces. A number plate can be traced to a person, and the same is true of a distinctive vehicle at a known location.

The moment such footage becomes public, the personal rights of those shown come into play. The question is not whether the recording is genuine but whether there is a reason to show other people publicly without their consent.

## The purpose has to match the reach

A scene showing a typical hazard and alerting other road users has a recognisable purpose. Footage that mainly serves to expose one individual does not.

The more strongly a publication targets a particular person, the more weight their interest in not being shown carries. That holds regardless of how irritating the behaviour was.

## Anonymise before it goes public

Faces and plates of uninvolved people belong blurred. The same applies to those involved, provided the scene stays understandable without identifying them, which is nearly always the case.

Make sure the blur tracks across the whole clip. A single frame in which the plate stays visible undoes the entire effort. The article on [blurring faces and plates](/en/blog/blurring-faces-and-plates) covers what matters.

## Context rather than commentary

Footage without context invites speculation. Anyone publishing should explain what can be seen without imputing intentions. Wording that anticipates a verdict shifts the discussion away from the substance.

State only what you know for certain. Estimated speeds and assumed motives are not information, they are claims.

## While proceedings are running

As long as a matter is with authorities, courts or insurers, publishing is not a good idea. It can prejudge those involved, and it can weaken your own position.

Wait until the matter is concluded. The footage loses nothing in the meantime.

## Publishing through a channel

If you offer your clip to a channel, that channel handles the treatment and the decision about publication. So look beforehand at how the channel deals with people in frame. The article on [discovering channels](/en/blog/discovering-channels) describes how to tell.

## What stays with you

Even after publication the original file remains yours. Keep it unchanged for as long as the matter has any relevance, and store it separately from the edited version.

That separation is more than tidiness. Anyone later asked whether a published recording matches the original can only answer if both versions exist.

## A simple test question

Imagine you were the person visible in the footage. Would the publication still seem reasonable to you? If the answer is no, it is worth reconsidering whether the scene really has to become public in that form.
