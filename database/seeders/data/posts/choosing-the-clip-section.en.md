A scene you lived through looks different to someone who only sees the clip. You know what to watch for. A first-time viewer does not. How you trim the footage therefore decides whether your recording makes sense at all.

## The lead-in carries the scene

The most important rule: start earlier than you think. Roughly ten seconds before the actual event is enough in most cases. During that time the situation builds up, and that build-up is the difference between a sequence someone can follow and a collision that appears out of nowhere.

An example: a vehicle moves into your lane without indicating. Without a lead-in, a viewer sees a car that is suddenly there. With one, they see it approaching over several seconds and that no indicator came on.

## The tail can stay short

Five seconds after the event are usually enough. They show how the situation resolved and close the scene. Anything beyond that is filler.

There is one exception: if something relevant happens immediately afterwards, it belongs in the clip. A vehicle that does not stop, a third party swerving, a second impact. Such continuations explain the consequences and are rarely superfluous.

> [!NOTE]
> Never cut into or out of the middle of a movement. A cut at the exact moment of impact feels as though something is missing, even when nothing is.

## When the scene is split

Some cameras break recordings into segments of one or three minutes. If your event sits right on a boundary, you need both files. Join them before trimming, otherwise the very transition that matters is the part that disappears.

Leave the timestamp untouched in that case. It shows that the two segments genuinely follow one another.

## What not to do while trimming

Change nothing about the image itself. No slow motion at the decisive point, no arrows, no music, no overlaid commentary. Such additions read as an interpretation, and anyone assessing the footage wants to see what is there before the interpretation.

Save the section without re-encoding where you can. Every extra round of compression costs detail, and detail is the reason you are submitting the scene at all. There is more on this in the article about [file formats and resolution](/en/blog/file-formats-and-resolution).

Keep the untrimmed original as well. If someone later wants to see the full sequence, you still have it.

## Several events on one drive

Sometimes more than one thing worth mentioning happens on a single route. Resist the temptation to pack everything into one long clip. Two separate submissions are almost always better than one with four minutes of uneventful country road between two scenes.

Separate clips can be assessed independently, declined individually and used individually. You can also describe each one on its own terms instead of explaining two different situations in a single text.

The exception is events that belong together, such as a swerve and the collision that follows from it. Those stay in one clip, because they only make sense as a sequence.
