Following too closely is hard to describe and easy to film. That is exactly why it is one of the situations where footage genuinely contributes something, provided it shows the right things.

## What footage can establish

Three things are visible: the gap over time, how long the situation lasted and the behaviour of the other vehicle, such as flashing headlights, repeatedly closing up or overtaking dangerously.

Duration matters particularly. Closing up briefly over a crest is not the same as a vehicle staying close for several kilometres. Footage covering two minutes therefore says more than a ten second excerpt.

## What it cannot establish

The exact gap in metres. A camera distorts distances, and a rear camera with a wide field of view makes a vehicle look further away than it is.

Nor does footage show intent. Whether someone is deliberately pressuring you or simply driving inattentively close cannot be distinguished from outside, and you should not attempt that distinction in the description either.

> [!NOTE]
> Describe what can be seen and leave the assessment to others. A factual description holds up considerably better than one that anticipates a verdict.

## The rear camera is decisive here

Without a view behind there is nothing to show. Anyone regularly driving busy routes gains from a second camera in exactly these situations, as the article on [front, rear and interior](/en/blog/front-rear-interior) describes.

Watch the cleanliness of the rear window. Tinted glass, dirt and the absence of a wiper often make rear footage unusable, particularly in rain.

## How to behave

The most important point first: footage is no reason to maintain the situation. Move over, let the vehicle past and put distance between you. Corrective measures such as braking or slowing down are dangerous and legally risky.

Secure the recording afterwards if you want to keep it. The impact sensor does not trigger here, because nothing strikes anything.

## When a report is appropriate

Where there was concrete danger, for instance over a long stretch at high speed with a very small gap, a report can be appropriate. What to consider is described in the article on [presenting footage to the police](/en/blog/presenting-footage-to-the-police).

For everyday close following without further circumstances, a report is rarely the right route. Footage showing a typical situation, on the other hand, interests a channel covering road safety.

## Choosing the right section

Because duration is what counts here, the usual time window is too short. Submit the full period during which the situation lasted, even if that runs to two or three minutes.

Start where the vehicle closes up and end where it turns off or overtakes. Everything in between belongs to the scene, even if it looks much the same throughout.

## What belongs in the description

The road and direction of travel, your approximate speed from the speedometer, the duration and your own behaviour. If you moved over and the gap stayed the same, that is the most important information in the whole scene.
