Two cameras deliver two perspectives, but only produce a gain if the recordings can be brought together. The most common reason that fails is mundane: two clocks that do not agree.

## Why clocks drift apart

Every camera has its own clock, and each one runs slightly inaccurately. Over months that adds up to minutes. Add a power loss and one of them may fall back to a default date.

Devices with position reception set their clock automatically. If only one of your cameras has that reception, the two still drift apart, in a direction nobody expects.

## Aligning the clocks

Set both cameras on the same day from the same source, such as your phone. Note when you did it.

Repeat the alignment on a fixed rhythm. The [monthly quick check](/en/blog/your-monthly-dashcam-check) is the right occasion, and ten seconds per camera is enough.

> [!NOTE]
> If you find a deviation, correct it but note the old state. Recordings made before the correction still carry the old time.

## A reference point in the image

There is a trick that works without aligning clocks: an event both cameras see. A firm braking manoeuvre, an indicator, crossing a speed bump.

If you identify the same moment in both recordings, you can align them on it regardless of what the timestamps say. That is the method that works most reliably in practice.

## Two cards, two sets of files

Keep the recordings from both cameras separate but name them consistently. The same file name with a suffix for the perspective is enough, as described in the article on [your clip archive](/en/blog/your-clip-archive).

After an event, always copy both files, even if you only need one at first. The second perspective cannot be obtained later once the loop has moved on.

## When submitting and for insurers

Submit both perspectives separately and mention the other in each description. The same applies for insurers and authorities: both original files, unchanged, with a note about the shared period. The article on [combining clips from several cameras](/en/blog/combining-clips-from-several-cameras) covers the details.

## Expect different image quality

Front and rear cameras rarely deliver the same picture. The rear window is often tinted, is not wiped and gets dirty faster, and many rear cameras are technically simpler.

So do not expect both recordings to be equally usable. For matching them up the weaker perspective is usually still enough, because what counts there is movement and order rather than fine detail.

## When one of the two fails

That happens more often than you would think and goes unnoticed for a long time without checks. During your quick check, look at both cameras explicitly, not only the front one.

A simple test: a short drive, then open the most recent file from each card. If both play, the system is working.

## One system or two devices

Systems where the rear camera connects to the front one solve the time problem by themselves, because only one clock is involved. Two independent devices are more flexible but demand regular alignment.
