Many dashcams record position and speed alongside the image. Some overlay those values directly onto the picture, others store them in a companion file. Both have a benefit and a downside worth knowing.

## What location data contributes

It answers questions that otherwise stay open. Where exactly did the scene happen? Which direction were you travelling? How fast were you immediately beforehand?

Speed in particular is a recurring point of dispute in collision cases. A recorded figure does not replace an expert assessment, but it does prevent crude misjudgements in either direction.

Location saves work too. Anyone describing a submission can take the place straight from the recording instead of reconstructing it from memory later.

## Where the risk sits

A position reveals more than the location of one scene. Across several recordings a movement profile emerges: when you travel where, which route you drive daily, where you live and work.

While that data stays on your card and gets overwritten, it is uncritical. The moment you pass a recording on, you pass that information on as well.

> [!NOTE]
> An overlaid speed display documents your own behaviour too. Anyone travelling above the limit supplies that evidence along with everything else.

## A measured setting

For normal operation, recording it makes sense. The benefit when a claim arises outweighs the downside, and the data disappears with the recording loop anyway.

Before passing footage on, however, it is worth checking what travels with it. For insurers and authorities the data belongs there, because it helps establish what happened. For a submission to a channel that may later be published, the balance is different.

## Overlay or companion file

An overlay in the image cannot be removed afterwards without altering the picture. A companion file can be handled separately: you pass it on when it is needed and leave it out when it is not.

If your camera gives you the choice, the companion file is the more flexible option. Remember to secure it together with the video, otherwise it is missing later.

## When travelling abroad

In countries with strict rules on video recording, position logging is viewed critically as well. Anyone driving there should run the camera sparingly in any case; the article on [using a dashcam abroad](/en/blog/dashcams-abroad) collects what matters.

## Time matters more than position

If you have to choose between the two, the correct clock is the more valuable. A location can be reconstructed from the surroundings in the image; a clock set wrong cannot.

Many cameras set their clock automatically from the position signal. That is a useful side effect of location logging: as long as reception holds, the time stays correct even after a power loss or a battery change.

## A quick check

Take a look at what your camera actually writes. Open any recording and watch for overlaid values, then check the card for files sitting alongside the video. After that you know what you pass on when you pass a recording on.
