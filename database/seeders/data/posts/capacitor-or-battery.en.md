The specifications of a dashcam contain one detail that is barely advertised and yet decides how long the device lasts: whether the camera uses a capacitor or a battery as its energy store.

## What the energy store is actually for

Both components have the same job, and it is smaller than most people assume. The store does not keep the camera running for hours. It supplies just enough energy to close the current file cleanly when power disappears.

That is the decisive function. If power cuts out mid-recording without the file being closed properly, you end up with a corrupted segment that will not open afterwards. That is a problem precisely after a crash, where a fuse may have blown.

## Why capacitors age better

A battery stores energy chemically. That chemistry is sensitive to temperature. Heat accelerates ageing, frost reduces the available capacity, and after a year or two behind a windscreen little of the original performance remains.

In the worst case an aged battery swells. The housing distorts, the camera no longer seats properly, and the mount sits crooked. This is exactly why many manufacturers now advise against batteries in vehicle cameras themselves.

A capacitor stores energy electrically. It tolerates high and low temperatures considerably better, ages more slowly and does not lose its function gradually.

> [!NOTE]
> A capacitor is not enough to run the camera without vehicle power. If you want recording while parked, you need a permanent supply regardless of the energy store.

## How to tell which one is fitted

The specifications either give a figure in milliamp hours, which points to a battery, or mention a capacitor. If neither appears, it is worth asking the manufacturer. Very cheap devices often stay quiet on this point, and there is usually a battery inside.

Behaviour in use is another clue. If the camera keeps running for minutes after the engine stops and can be operated without vehicle power, a battery is fitted. If it switches off after a few seconds, that points to a capacitor.

## What it means day to day

If you have the choice, take the capacitor. The price difference is small, reliability over the years is noticeably better, and you spare yourself the question of whether the device still does its job after a third summer.

If you already own a camera with a battery, that is no reason to replace it immediately. Do check regularly that the housing is still flat and that a file is actually created after a cold start. There is more in the article on [heat, cold and humidity](/en/blog/heat-cold-and-humidity).

## What to watch for in summer

On hot days the interior of a parked vehicle reaches temperatures at which many devices shut down. That is a protective mechanism rather than a fault. It becomes a problem when the camera does not resume by itself once things cool off.

So in high summer, check after a long stay in the sun that recording has actually restarted. A device with a capacitor tends to survive such cycles for longer, but it is worth a look there too.

Take the camera with you during very long stops in the sun. That protects the hardware and removes the incentive to break into a vehicle with visible electronics inside.
