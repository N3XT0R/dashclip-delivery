The memory card is the cheapest component in a dashcam and the one most recordings fail on. The reason is simple: a dashcam writes without pause, and ordinary cards are not built for that constant load.

## Why cards die in dashcams

Memory cells tolerate a limited number of write cycles. A card in a stills camera never reaches that limit. In a dashcam the same card is written full and overwritten several times a day.

Temperature adds to it. Behind a windscreen, summer conditions accelerate the ageing further. After a few months read errors accumulate, segments break off, and eventually the camera stops recording altogether.

The awkward part is that the failure is usually silent. The camera still shows a picture and the indicator still lights up, but no usable file is produced any more.

## What to look for when buying

Choose a card explicitly rated for continuous write load. Manufacturers usually label such cards as high endurance and mention them in the context of surveillance and vehicle cameras.

Check the write speed class as well. For high resolution recording the card should sustain at least thirty megabytes per second. On capacity the rule is: enough for a day of driving, not more. Larger cards only extend the period you keep material you do not need.

> [!NOTE]
> Check the maximum capacity your camera supports. A larger card is otherwise not recognised at all, or only partly.

## Looking after it

Format the card in the camera, not on a computer. Only that produces the file system the camera works with reliably. Repeat it every one to two months, because formatting also clears the protected files that would otherwise occupy space permanently.

Back up whatever you want to keep beforehand. Formatting does not ask.

## The test that counts

Take two minutes once a month: switch the camera on, record briefly, then open the file on a computer or in the app. If it plays, the chain of camera, card and file system is working.

Replace the card as soon as errors appear, and expect to change it roughly once a year regardless. Measured against what a missing recording costs when it matters, that is a small amount. The article on the [monthly quick check](/en/blog/your-monthly-dashcam-check) collects this routine.

## How to spot a dying card

There are warning signs if you watch for them. The camera takes longer after starting before it records. Individual files will not play or break off mid-frame. The number of files on the card does not match the time you drove. Occasionally a card error message appears and disappears again after a restart.

Each of those is a reason to replace the card rather than format it once more. A card that has shown errors does not become reliable again.

## Two cards in rotation

If you want to be safe, keep a second card ready. After an incident you take the written card out, insert the spare and carry on immediately with a working camera. The original card stays untouched until you have copied the file at your leisure.

That costs a few euros and solves the problem that otherwise occurs most often: a camera sitting in the vehicle for days with no card in it after an incident.
