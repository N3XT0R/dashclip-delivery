Two details decide whether footage is more than moving pictures: when it was recorded and where. Both sound trivial and both get lost regularly in practice.

## The clock is the more important of the two

A location can be reconstructed from the image if necessary: signs, distinctive buildings, the surroundings. A wrong time cannot be reconstructed, and it casts doubt on the whole recording.

Cameras that fall back to a default date after a power loss are particularly treacherous. Anyone who fails to notice suddenly has footage dated the first of January in a year long past.

## Clock changes and time zones

Few cameras follow the switch between summer and winter time automatically. After the change the clock is an hour out, and nobody notices for months.

Travelling abroad adds the time zone. Set the camera before you leave, or note the deviation so you can state it later.

> [!NOTE]
> A camera clock that deviates is not a problem as long as you know the deviation and state it. It becomes a problem when someone else discovers the discrepancy.

## The location in the description

An approximate statement is enough: town and street, or a motorway section with direction of travel. Coordinates to the metre are rarely necessary and reveal more than the scene needs.

Include the direction of travel. Without it, nobody can tell which carriageway is meant on a motorway, and that changes how a scene is read considerably.

## Data recorded automatically

Many cameras write position and speed into the image or into a companion file. That is practical, because you can take the details straight from it. What else travels with it is covered in the article on [location data](/en/blog/location-data).

Even so, do not rely on it alone. Reception drops in cities and tunnels, and that is exactly when the detail you need is missing.

## The habit that solves all of it

Check your camera clock at every quick check. It takes ten seconds and prevents the one case where otherwise good footage fails on a detail.

Note the place and time by hand after an event as well, while you still remember them. In two weeks your note is worth more than your memory.
## When the details are missing

It happens that you watch footage weeks later and know neither the date nor the place for certain. Do not throw it away for that reason, but write into the description what you can say for sure and mark the rest as uncertain.

Honest uncertainty is far better than invented precision. A channel can work with the statement that a scene happened in late autumn on a country road outside town. Nobody can work with a wrong time.

## A habit worth building

Say the place and time out loud after an event if your phone is recording anyway, or stop at the next lay-by and type two lines. That costs a minute and replaces any later reconstruction.
