Not every loss involves other road users. A deer bolting from the undergrowth, a stone from the bed of a lorry, a marten that has made itself at home in the engine bay overnight: footage can help here too, though differently than in a classic collision.

## Wildlife collisions

In a collision with an animal, fault is rarely the issue; proving that it genuinely was a wildlife collision is. Footage shows the sequence, the time of day, the visibility and that no other vehicle was involved.

That helps with an insurer but does not replace the usual steps: make the scene safe, notify the police or the responsible body and obtain confirmation of the wildlife collision.

> [!WARNING]
> An injured animal is not a case for improvisation. Notify the responsible body and wait for instructions.

## Stone chips

Here footage is often disappointing. The stone is small, fast and barely visible in the image. What you can see is the context: a lorry ahead with an unsecured load, roadworks with loose gravel, the moment of impact in the audio.

Secure the recording anyway if a specific vehicle is in question. Combined with a readable plate it can become more than your own loss. What that takes is described in the article on [readable number plates](/en/blog/readable-number-plates).

## Animal damage and damage while parked

A front camera is generally no help here. The damage occurs under the bonnet and at night, outside any frame.

Parking mode reaches its limits as well: it records what happens in front of the vehicle, not inside it. Expect no documentation here and rely on other measures.

## What these cases have in common

In all three, footage is supporting evidence rather than the main proof. Photographs of the damage, a prompt report and the usual documentation remain decisive.

Secure the recording promptly nonetheless, because it is the only part that cannot be reproduced later. How to do that is in the article on [rescuing footage](/en/blog/rescuing-footage-before-it-is-overwritten).

## Suitable for a submission?

Wildlife crossings and falling loads are subjects that affect other drivers directly, particularly at dusk and in the season when animals cross roads more often. Such footage interests channels, provided the scene is understandable and no animal is shown in a way that helps nobody.

## Prevention beats documentation

With wildlife, speed is the main factor. Reducing speed on country roads at dusk and watching the verges prevents more than any recording could ever prove.

With stone chips, distance is the lever. Behind vehicles with open beds and on freshly gravelled sections, more distance is the only effective measure.

## What to record after damage

Photographs from several distances, the exact spot on the vehicle, the date and time, the location and the weather. If footage exists, note as well where in the video the event occurs.
