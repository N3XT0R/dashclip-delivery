Video footage feels objective. It shows what happened, without opinion and without gaps in memory. That impression misleads regularly, because a camera only ever shows a section, and that section is distorted.

## The field of view deceives about distance

Wide angle lenses, which sit in almost every dashcam, make distances look greater than they are. A vehicle following closely looks more harmless in the footage than it did in the mirror.

Conversely, objects at the edge of the frame appear stretched and faster than they actually were. Anyone deriving speeds from footage needs reference points in the image rather than a feeling.

## The blind spot stays blind

A front camera looks forwards. What happens alongside the vehicle is not in frame, and that is exactly where many situations occur: the car pulling out of a parking space, the bicycle passing a queue on the right.

The front of your own vehicle is rarely visible either. Whether an impact involved the left or right corner is usually not something footage shows.

## Light and contrast set limits

Against the light, everything outside the bright area disappears. In darkness the image reaches as far as the headlights. A pedestrian in dark clothing may only appear in the footage once they are lit, although the human eye would have picked them up earlier.

> [!NOTE]
> What is not visible in the footage was not necessarily invisible. The reverse holds too: what the camera shows, you may not have perceived while driving.

## Audio is almost always missing

Anyone who switched the microphone off, which is the recommendation for good reasons, loses cues such as a horn or screeching tyres. That is a deliberate trade-off, and one worth remembering when you assess footage.

## What follows from this

Footage is a powerful aid and not proof of everything. It establishes order, positions and behaviour within the visible area, and it says nothing about the rest.

Word your description carefully in line with that. Writing what can be seen keeps you on solid ground. Deriving from the image what is not in it gets corrected as soon as somebody looks more closely. The article on [writing the description](/en/blog/writing-the-description) goes into it.

## Two recordings show more than one

Because every perspective has blind spots, a second camera provides not only more picture but a cross-check. What looks like a sudden manoeuvre in one recording turns out to be a signalled lane change in the other.

That requires a shared time base, as described in the article on [running two cameras in sync](/en/blog/running-two-cameras-in-sync).

## How to test the limits

Drive a route you know and watch the footage afterwards. Note from what distance you can make out detail, how wide the covered area actually is and where the edge of the frame falls.

After that you know what your camera can show, which is a far more reliable basis than assuming it sees everything.
