Footage can shorten the handling of a claim considerably. Instead of conflicting accounts there is a sequence someone can watch. For that to work, how you hand the file over matters.

## The original, nothing else

Submit the unchanged file exactly as the camera wrote it. No trimming, no conversion, no blurring, no overlaid arrows.

The reason is simple: an edited file raises the question of what else was changed. If you also want to provide a short excerpt, add it alongside the original rather than in place of it.

Keep a copy of your own as well. Handing over does not mean giving away.

## What to supply with it

A short written note helps the handler understand the footage: date, time, place, direction of travel and where in the video the event occurs. A pointer such as "at 34 seconds" saves repeated searching.

Mention any deviation of the camera clock if there is one. Volunteering that takes the ground out from under a later dispute.

> [!NOTE]
> If several cameras were involved, supply both files and point out that they cover the same period. The article on [running two cameras in sync](/en/blog/running-two-cameras-in-sync) covers what to watch for.

## The right moment

Mention the footage when you report the claim. Producing it four weeks later means explaining why. By then the file may also have been overwritten long ago if you did not secure it.

So secure the recording before you make the call. How to do that is described in the article on [rescuing footage](/en/blog/rescuing-footage-before-it-is-overwritten).

## What footage does not replace

The usual steps remain necessary: photographs of the final positions and the damage, the details of everyone involved, and an accident statement where appropriate. Footage shows the sequence but not the extent of the damage, and not who stood where once it was over.

Expect as well that footage does not necessarily speak in your favour. It shows what happened, including your own mistakes.

## The data protection side

Passing footage to an insurer serves the handling of a specific claim. That is a different case from publication and considerably easier to justify. Even so, hand over only the recording in question, not your entire memory card.

What applies to publication is covered in the article on [publishing footage](/en/blog/publishing-footage).

## The handover itself

Ask which route the file is expected to take. Many insurers offer an upload in their customer area, others accept a storage medium. Sending it as an email attachment often fails on file size, and shrinking the file is not an option.

Name the file traceably, for instance with the date and the claim number, and leave the original untouched alongside it. Note when you handed which file to whom.

## When the other party has footage too

The other side may have run a camera as well. A fuller picture emerges in that case, which is fundamentally a good thing, regardless of whom it favours.

Stick to the same approach: submit the original, state any deviations, and keep your own assessments out of the description.
