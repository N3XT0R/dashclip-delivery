In daylight almost every camera produces a usable image. The difference between devices shows after dark, and that is where it is decided whether footage is worth anything when it counts.

## What happens in darkness

A camera sensor needs light. Where there is none, the automatics raise sensitivity. That brightens the image and amplifies noise at the same time, meaning the fine disturbances that swallow detail. In parallel the camera lengthens the exposure, which blurs moving objects.

Together those effects mean a number plate readable from twenty metres in daylight is already unreadable from ten metres at night.

## What matters in the camera

Two specifications decide it, and both are rarely advertised: the speed of the lens and the size of the sensor. A fast lens lets more light through, a larger sensor collects more of it per pixel. Both matter more than any label for a night vision feature.

A high resolution figure helps little at night. More pixels on the same sensor area means smaller pixels, and smaller pixels gather less light. A camera with moderate resolution and a good sensor often delivers the better night result.

## The biggest problem is glare

It is not darkness that makes footage unusable, it is contrast. Oncoming headlights are many times brighter than the rest of the scene. The automatics respond by darkening, and everything outside the beam disappears.

Technology helps only so far against that. What you can influence is the glass: every bit of dirt scatters light further and creates a veil across the whole image. A windscreen clean on both sides achieves more at night than any change of setting.

> [!NOTE]
> Reflections from inside the cabin ruin night footage too. Bright objects on the dashboard, a glowing display or an interior light appear as a ghost image in the recording.

## What to expect realistically

With good street lighting, sequences are easy to follow at night and plates are often readable. On an unlit country road, footage essentially shows what your own headlights reach, and movement at the roadside stays a silhouette.

That is not a shortcoming of your camera, it is a physical limit. For assessing how a collision unfolded it is usually still enough, because what matters there is positions and order rather than fine detail.

## Settings for the dark

Leave exposure on the default unless your camera offers a deliberate correction. Lowering it by one step helps where there is a lot of oncoming light, because highlights blow out less.

Check the frame rate as well. More frames per second mean shorter exposures and therefore darker night images. Thirty frames per second is the better compromise, as the article on [resolution, bitrate and frame rate](/en/blog/resolution-bitrate-frame-rate) explains.

## The simplest test

Drive a route you know well after dark and watch the recording afterwards at your leisure. Note from what distance a number plate stays readable and whether pedestrians at the roadside are recognisable.

That result is your realistic expectation for a real incident. It helps more than any marketing copy, and it shows you whether adjusting alignment or settings is worthwhile.
