Every box states a resolution, and many purchase decisions end right there. In fact three values together describe what footage is worth: resolution, bitrate and frame rate. The best known of them says the least.

## Resolution: how many pixels

Resolution states how many pixels make up a single frame. More pixels generally mean more detail, and a number plate in the background is more likely to stay readable.

The catch: pixels alone say nothing about their quality. If the lens or the sensor cannot keep up, you get a high resolution but soft image. The file grows larger while the information in it stays the same.

## Bitrate: how much data per second

Bitrate describes how much data is stored per second. It decides how much of what the sensor sees actually lands on the card.

A low bitrate is barely noticeable when nothing moves. As soon as something does, the image breaks into blocks, edges smear and text becomes illegible. That is exactly the situation, movement, in which you need the recording.

As a guide, full high definition footage sits in a usable range at roughly fifteen to twenty-five megabits per second. If the specifications state considerably less, the camera is economising in the wrong place.

> [!NOTE]
> High resolution combined with a low bitrate is the most common reason for unreadable plates. When in doubt, one step less resolution at the same bitrate is the better choice.

## Frame rate: how many frames per second

The frame rate determines how finely movement is resolved. Thirty frames per second is the usual value and sufficient for most situations.

More frames per second give sharper stills during fast movement but cost bitrate if the camera does not raise it accordingly. At sixty frames per second with unchanged bitrate, each individual frame receives only half the data.

One special case is the flicker of traffic lights and modern vehicle lighting. Depending on the frame rate such lights appear dark in the recording. If your camera offers a mains frequency setting, it should be set to fifty hertz in Europe.

## What to set in practice

Choose the full resolution of your camera, the highest bitrate offered and thirty frames per second. If the card fills too quickly as a result, reduce the resolution first, not the bitrate.

Then check the result on a still frame: a number plate around twenty metres away should be readable when stationary. If it is not, the article on [capturing readable number plates](/en/blog/readable-number-plates) takes it further.

## Why more is not always better

Raising any of these values costs storage, and storage is limited. A card that holds twelve hours at medium settings may hold four at maximum. For the loop that is no problem, since it overwrites anyway. It matters when you secure a recording only days later.

The camera itself has limits too. At maximum settings some devices run noticeably warmer, and heat is the most common cause of dropouts in high summer.

## The comparison that actually helps

Datasheets say less than a test of your own. Record the same route twice, once at maximum and once at medium settings, and look at both recordings at the same point. If you cannot see a difference, nothing speaks against the more economical option.

Pay particular attention to oncoming vehicles. That is where movement, changing light and fine detail meet, and where you find out whether a setting holds up.
