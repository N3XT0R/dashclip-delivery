Anonymising sounds like a single operation and is in practice where most goes wrong. A half-hearted edit looks like protection without being any, and that is more dangerous than no edit at all.

## What gets anonymised

Faces and number plates are the standard case. Added to those are features that identify someone just as clearly: company lettering on a vehicle, a house number beside the scene, a distinctive vehicle at a known location.

What does not get anonymised is the subject of the scene. Blurring the other party completely leaves you with a video that says nothing.

## The most common mistake

A pixelation that sits on one frame while the object moves. After a few frames the vehicle runs out of the mask and the plate is readable again.

The mask therefore has to follow the object. Software offers tracking for that, but it needs checking: through corners, behind obstructions and during fast movement, tracking regularly loses its target.

> [!NOTE]
> Review the result frame by frame, at least where the object moves quickly. A single unedited frame is enough to undo the entire effort.

## Pixelate, blur or cover

Coarse pixelation is safer than a light blur. Blurred plates can under some circumstances be reconstructed, because the original information is still present in the image.

Safest of all is an opaque shape. It looks less elegant but leaves nothing behind that could be computed back.

## Audio belongs in this

Perfectly anonymised footage helps little if a name or a plate is spoken on the soundtrack. Listen to the audio before considering the edit finished, or remove it entirely.

## The original stays untouched

Always edit a copy. You need the original unchanged if the footage matters to an insurer or the authorities. An anonymised version does not belong there.

Keep both versions apart and name them clearly so you do not mix them up later. The articles on [submitting footage to insurers](/en/blog/submitting-footage-to-insurers) and on [presenting footage to the police](/en/blog/presenting-footage-to-the-police) explain why that matters.

## If you have no software for it

Then the better route is to trim the clip so that uninvolved people do not appear clearly in the first place, and to leave the editing to the channel that decides about publication.

## The effort in practice

For a twenty second clip with two or three objects to treat, allow twenty to thirty minutes if you work carefully. Most of that goes not into setting the masks but into checking them.

Plan for that time or leave the job alone. An edit that is half finished creates the impression of care without delivering it.

## A short checklist

Does every mask follow its object across the whole clip? Does the scene stay understandable despite the edit? Is the resolution unchanged? Is the untouched original still stored separately?

If you answer yes to those four questions, the version is ready for publication.
