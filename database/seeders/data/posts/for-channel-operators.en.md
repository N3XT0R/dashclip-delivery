Most articles here address people submitting clips. This one changes perspective: what does the platform look like when you run a channel and receive material?

## Getting the role

As a channel operator you also start with an ordinary [registration](/standard/register). From inside your account you then request the role. That is deliberate, because a channel is not a separate kind of login but an additional permission on an existing account.

Once it is granted, an overview of incoming offers appears in your area. Everything submitters have offered your channel collects there.

## Reviewing offers

An offer is an invitation, not an assignment. You see the clip, the description written by the submitter and the key details, and you decide whether the material suits your channel.

Three routes are open. You download the clip and work on from it. You return it because it does not fit. Or you let the deadline pass, which has the same effect as returning it, only slower.

Returning actively is the better choice. It releases the clip earlier so another channel can be offered it, and it is the clearer response towards the submitter.

> [!NOTE]
> A download creates no obligation to publish. It only means you have reviewed the material and taken it on.

## Why not everything is offered at once

A clip is not made available to all selected channels at the same time. Distribution runs in sequence instead, so several channels do not work on the same scene in parallel and publish identical footage at the end. The details are explained in the article on [automatic video distribution](/en/blog/automatic-video-distribution-dashclip-delivery).

For you that means the sooner you review, the more material is available to you, and the less often an interesting clip moves on before you have seen it.

## Care that shows

Submitters watch what happens to their material. A channel that blurs plates and faces, puts scenes into context and does not hide where a clip came from is offered better material over time.

The opposite travels just as fast when footage is used without context and without regard for bystanders. The article on [publishing footage](/en/blog/publishing-footage) collects what to watch for.

## Keeping track

Record which clips you downloaded and which you actually used. That saves duplicated work and helps when a submitter asks what became of their recording.

## Responses that help submitters

You do not have to justify a rejection, but one sentence makes a difference. Someone who learns that the lead-in was missing, or that a similar subject was published only recently, sends something more suitable next time.

Over time that builds a circle of submitters whose material regularly suits your channel. It is considerably more effective than waiting for as many offers as possible.

## Deadlines as a tool

The time limit on an offer works for both sides. It stops material sitting with a channel that currently has no capacity, and it gives you a clear frame for your own review.

If you know you will not get to it in the next few days, returning early is the cleaner solution. What happens in the background is described in the article on [offer deadlines](/en/blog/understanding-offer-deadlines).
